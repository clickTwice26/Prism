This is Mitu. Her class is learning photosynthesis. Mitu is deaf, and the lesson isn't reaching her. Bangla Sign Language has been official since two thousand nine, yet most deaf children still learn from spoken lessons. There are too few interpreters, and many science words have no sign at all. And it isn't only Bangladesh: about seventy million deaf people worldwide use sign languages, and most classrooms aren't built for them.

Prism Sign brings the same lesson to deaf learners, in Bangla Sign Language for the children, their families, their teachers and interpreters. What makes it different? AI does the routine work. Real people do the signing, and deaf reviewers approve every sign. Recognition runs in the browser, so no video ever leaves the device.

In Lessons, every key word with an approved sign is underlined. Tap it and the sign plays. An interpreter window can be moved, resized or hidden, and captions stay on. For about three thousand five hundred documented words no one has recorded yet, a 3D signer shows a labelled preview. Most deaf children have hearing parents. In Sign words, a parent signs, and Prism checks the handshape, place, movement and direction, then says exactly what to fix. Fingerspell teaches the Bangla two-hand alphabet the same way, letter by letter. And in a live class, captions appear as the teacher speaks, key terms link to their signs, and an interpreter can join from anywhere.

Prism uses three kinds of AI. A Gemini language model finds each lesson's key terms in Bangla, English and Malay. Speech recognition turns the teacher's voice into captions. And MediaPipe tracks hand and body points in the browser, which Prism compares, part by part, with about three thousand six hundred documented signs. So it's built from written sign descriptions, not a huge video dataset.

In our pilot, we'll measure what matters: ninety percent of each lesson's key terms signed, a comprehension gain for most children, and a rating of four out of five from deaf learners themselves.
